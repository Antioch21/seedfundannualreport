As I look back on the last 3 years, my heart is truly filled with thanksgiving to God.

First came the casting of the vision to create a ‘seed fund’ for missionary support, by the late Joseph Chean in 2023.

Then came a year of having many conversations with various people to flesh out the concept for the Missions Seed Fund (MSF).

Following that was a year of pilot-testing, leading to the official launch of the MSF in 2025.

And now here we are.

In two years, over 160 donors and 7 churches have together raised over $547,651 — enabling us to partner with 10 churches and deploy 30 workers across 11 countries in 6 regions.

This update is a review of the work done with the MSF.

We mean for it to testify to three things:

  1. How the Lord is raising more laborers for His global harvest field from out of the Singapore Church;
  2. How we have had the opportunity to come alongside many local churches to do a better work of missionary-sending; and
  3. How the Lord has stirred generosity from the hearts of people across the Church.

As you read it, may your heart overflow with thanksgiving!

And may the Lord continue to bless the work of our hands, that His name be made known among the nations!

The Quiet Pilot

The Seed Fund began quietly, running two pilot grant calls to test our processes, sharpen our assessment criteria, and learn what churches actually need.

Phase 2 · Oct 2025 – Sept 2026

Official Launch

On 2 October 2025, the Seed Fund was publicly launched at the Antioch Summit. A third grant call — our first official one — opened to the wider church community.

How the Seed Fund Works

The Seed Fund is a partnership model built around three commitments:

Church-Led Sending

Applications must come through a committed local church. We partner with the church, not just the individual — ensuring workers are sent with community, accountability, and long-term holistic support. Workers in partnership with missions agencies can also be sent, provided the local church is the primary applicant, working in close partnership with the agency.

Catalytic Funding

We provide up to one-third of livelihood costs for up to two years — capped at $1,000/month for singles and $3,000/month for families. This is seed capital, not full support. The church and other partners carry the rest.

Strategic Focus

Priority is given to deployments among unreached people groups — populations where less than 2% are evangelical Christians.

Stewardship & Governance

Applications are assessed by an independent Seed Fund Advisory Committee (SFAC), composed of experienced practitioners in missions and church leadership.

Impact in the Field

Workers are active across Southeast Asia, South Asia, Africa, East Asia, West Asia, and Central Asia. The work they do varies, but centres on:

Church Planting and Community Formation

Establishing worshipping communities among people with little or no access to the gospel. This is long-horizon work — measured in years and relationships, not quarterly outputs.

Language and Cultural Immersion

Many workers in their first one to two years are primarily engaged in language acquisition and cross-cultural integration. Sustainable, long-term presence begins here.

Bi-Vocational Engagement

Several workers serve through professional roles — in education, business, healthcare, in both for and non-profit settings — which both sustain their presence in those contexts and create genuine points of connection with local communities.

Partnerships with Local Believers

Where local church networks exist, our workers seek to strengthen rather than supplant them — building relationships with indigenous leaders and supporting locally-rooted ministry.

What We Can and Cannot Report

We want to be honest with you about the limits of what we know at this stage.

For workers deployed in 2025, some are still in language learning and early integration. For all workers, the nature of cross-cultural work means that fruit — real, lasting fruit — often takes years to become visible.

What we can say: workers are on the ground, in relationship, and faithful to the calling they have been sent to pursue.

Learnings from
the Seed Fund Team

What We’ve Learnt Building the Seed Fund

Over two years of pilots, conversations, application reviews, and check-ins, our team has accumulated important learnings that we’d like to share with you. We share these not as criticisms of the model, but with a desire and an open invitation for deeper conversations on how we can better serve the wider body of Christ.

On the Application Process

Every church that came to us arrived with its own definition of what it means to send a worker — its own procedures, its own theology of calling, its own sense of who qualifies. Our aim was not to impose a single model but to create a rigorous, open process that helped us understand where each church was at, while humbly surfacing some of the important considerations in sending long-term cross-cultural workers well. The application fields were developed in consultation with experienced field workers, church sending committee members, and senior church leaders.

What we found was that the process opened genuine conversations — with smaller churches still developing their missions approach, and with larger churches willing to revisit models they had held for years. In almost every case, what motivated churches to engage was love for their own members: people sensing God’s call in ways that did not always fit existing policy. That love made churches willing to adapt, and sometimes to make real structural changes.

We learnt as much from these conversations as the churches did.

On Supporting Workers Well

Member care is crucial and non-negotiable. As we asked churches about their approach to supporting workers on the field, we also connected them with resources, agencies, and other churches with experience in this area. The peer-to-peer sharing this generated has been one of the more meaningful outcomes of the Seed Fund.

On the funding side: covering livelihood costs in the first two years matters more than it might seem. It frees workers to focus on language learning, preparation, and building relationships — rather than anxious fundraising — and gives them a firmer footing to raise the remaining support they need.

That said, we want to be honest about the limits of our current model. Covering livelihood costs only means that setup costs, ministry expenses, and the ad-hoc financial pressures that arise on the ground remain unaddressed. Workers and churches absorb these gaps themselves. We are exploring how to address this, and welcome conversations with donors who may wish to help us think it through.

On Raising and Deploying Funds

We have been genuinely moved by the breadth of giving to the Seed Fund. 7 churches from across the denominational spectrum have contributed about $180,000 — churches sowing into the missional efforts of other churches they may never have formally partnered with before. It is a small but real picture of the Acts 2 community: giving not along denominational lines, but in recognition of a shared mission.

Over 160 individual donors have also given through our Agathos fundraising page (from $10 to $1,000 and more) and directly, with gifts ranging from first-time contributions to individual gifts of $20,000 to $100,000. It tells us that the vision of a cross-denominational pooled fund resonates across a wide spectrum of the Singapore church — not only with major donors.

Together, these two streams have affirmed that it is possible to build a giving platform that cuts across denominations, deploys capital carefully, and earns people’s trust. We believe that paradigm has potential well beyond the Seed Fund’s current scope — and we are keen to explore how similar models might address other gaps in the missions landscape.

On the Bigger Picture

The Seed Fund addresses one gap — a significant one, but one among many.

What we support is the financial footing of new workers in their first two years: the stage most likely to prevent a deployment from happening at all. We are glad to fill this gap.

But as we have grown more embedded in the missions ecosystem, the other unaddressed needs have become harder to ignore — support for new field projects, care for workers returning after years of difficult service, resourcing for the local staff in agencies and churches who carry the work of sending but are rarely in anyone’s fundraising materials.

What the Seed Fund has shown, as an experiment, is that a cross-denominational platform for kingdom generosity can be both relational and rigorous. That model is worth building on. We do not know yet exactly what form that takes — but we are excited to find out, and we hope you will be part of that conversation.
